== Chapter 2 — Bank, Group, and Delegation Semantics

=== Scope

This chapter is the normative reference for the semantics of Groups,
Banks, Contracts, and delegation in the CE Suite. It specifies:

* The Group model: what a Group is, how it is identified, and how
ownership is enforced.
* Banks: their states, transitions, and ownership invariants.
* Contracts: their structure, hierarchy, and admission rules.
* Delegation: how resources move from parent to child ECID, and what
invariants hold at each step.
* Revocation: cooperative (`ec.ot`) and forced (`ec.oe`) paths.

Byte-level layouts are in Chapter 0. Instruction operand encodings are
in Chapter 3. The radix-tree allocation algorithms are in Appendix A.
This chapter defines what the operations mean; the other chapters define
how they are encoded.

'''''

=== 2.1 Groups

==== 2.1.1 GroupID = ECID

Every ECID `e` has exactly one Group. The Group’s identifier is the ECID
number itself: *GroupID = ECID*. There is no separate Group ID space, no
6-bit group number, and no instruction that creates a Group
independently of an ECID. When an ECID is allocated, its Group comes
into existence with it. When the ECID is destroyed, the Group is
destroyed with it.

This identity is not merely a convention. It is what makes hardware
enforcement O(1). Resources (Banks and Contracts) carry a *group owner
field* that stores the ECID number of their owning Group. To check
ownership, hardware reads that field and compares it to
`current_ecid` — one load, one comparison. If Groups had their own
ID space, hardware would need an additional translation step.

==== 2.1.2 Group as inventory, not container

A Group does not maintain a downward member list. It does not store a
list of "`which Banks belong to me.`" Instead, the relationship is
inverted:

* Each Bank stores the ECID number of the Group that owns it.
* Each Contract stores the ECID number of the Group that holds it.

To ask "`what does ECID `e` own?`" you search the Bank and Contract
tables for entries whose owner field equals `e`. To ask "`does ECID `e`
own Bank `b`?`" you read `b.owner` and compare. The latter question is
the one hardware asks at runtime, and it is answered in one instruction.

This is the *reversal trick*: ownership flows from resource to Group,
not from Group to resource. It converts membership queries from O(N)
(searching a member list) to O(1) (reading an up-pointer).

==== 2.1.3 Child Groups and visibility

When a parent ECID delegates resources to a child ECID, the child
receives those resources into its own Group — the Group whose GroupID
equals the child ECID number.

From the *child’s perspective*, the child has one Group and it is the
child’s own Group. The child cannot observe the host-level GroupID (its
ECID number is opaque to software running as that EC — see Chapter 1
§1.3), and cannot infer the ECID numbers or Group IDs of its parent,
siblings, or ancestors. Each delegation level renumbers its world to
start at zero.

From the *hardware’s perspective*, every Bank and Contract in the system
has an explicit ECID owner number. The hierarchy is tracked in the radix
tree (Appendix A) and in `EC[e].parent_ecid` (Chapter 0
§0.2). There is no ambiguity about who owns what.

'''''

=== 2.2 Banks

==== 2.2.1 Bank record

Every Bank has an owner field: the ECID number of the Group that
currently owns the Bank. The owner field is set when a Bank is assigned
to an ECID’s Group (via `ec.ig`) and cleared when the Bank is returned
to the free pool (via `ec.og` or `ec.oe`). The owner field is maintained
by the hardware; software cannot forge or overwrite it.

==== 2.2.2 Bank states

A Bank is always in exactly one of four states:

[width="100%",cols="16%,84%",options="header",]
|===
|State |Meaning
|*Free* |Not owned by any ECID. Available for assignment via `ec.ig`.

|*Owned* |Assigned to an ECID’s Group. The Bank’s owner field equals
that ECID.

|*Active* |Owned and currently in use by the running context
(`current_ecid`).

|*Sealed* |Owned and encrypted under hardware key management (`ec.iv`).
Contents inaccessible until unsealed by the owning EC.
|===

A Bank in the Free state has an undefined owner field. A Bank
transitions from Free to Owned via `ec.ig`. A Bank transitions from
Owned to Free via `ec.og` or as part of `ec.oe`. A Bank transitions to
Sealed via `ec.iv` and back to Owned via `ec.ov`.

==== 2.2.3 Bank types

*Non-VMT banks* hold general-purpose registers, floating-point
registers, selected CSRs, SATP, and cache partition configuration (CP).
Size: 1 KB on RV64, 512 B on RV32. Layout in Chapter 0 §0.4.

*VMT banks* hold vector, matrix, and tensor register files. Size is
implementation-defined but scales with vector width. VMT banks are
significantly larger than non-VMT banks and are typically fewer in
number.

Non-VMT and VMT banks are distinct pools. `ec.ig` and `ec.og` on a VMT
bank require that the target ECID has VMT delegation rights (specified
in `EC[e]`; see Chapter 0 §0.2). Implementations that do not
support VMT may omit VMT banks entirely.

==== 2.2.4 Bank ownership invariant

*A Bank may be owned by at most one ECID at any instant.* No instruction
sequence may cause a Bank’s owner field to simultaneously reflect two
different ECIDs, even transiently during a context switch. Ownership
transitions are atomic: the owner field changes from `old_ecid` to
`new_ecid` in a single hardware step.

A Bank cannot be accessed by an ECID other than its owner. Attempting to
issue `ec.ib` or `ec.ob` against a Bank owned by a different ECID raises
a trap (charter §6.6).

'''''

=== 2.3 Contracts

==== 2.3.1 Contract record

A Contract is a slice of a global multiplexed resource. The Contract
record holds:

* *Owner ECID*: the ECID number of the Group currently holding this
Contract.
* *Resource type*: MSE (memory bandwidth/latency), QoS (I/O bandwidth/
latency), or CPE (cache ways/fraction).
* *Allocation*: the amount of the resource allocated (bandwidth, latency
class, cache fraction — resource-type specific).
* *Parent Contract*: the contract from which this one was split, or null
for a root contract.
* *Delegation level*: the `L` value at which this Contract was created,
used to bound Contract trees by the same D as ECID delegation.

==== 2.3.2 Ownership and splitting

*Single ownership.* A Contract has exactly one owner at any instant. The
owner is identified by an ECID number in the Contract’s owner field —
the same up-pointer pattern as for Banks. Ownership can be transferred
but not duplicated.

*Hierarchical splitting.* A parent Contract may be split into child
Contracts. Each child is a strict subset of its parent’s allocation. The
sum of all children’s allocations must never exceed the parent’s
allocation. This invariant is enforced by hardware at the time of
splitting (atomic admission, §2.3.3). A Contract tree is bounded by the
same depth D as the ECID delegation tree; this matches the
virtualization hierarchy (§2.4.3).

*Assignment to a Group.* When a Contract is created or transferred, its
owner field is updated to the recipient ECID. The recipient’s Group now
"`holds`" the Contract via the same up-pointer mechanism as Banks: the
Contract points up to its owner ECID.

==== 2.3.3 Atomic admission

Splitting a Contract or assigning it to a new Group requires chip-global
arbitration. The arbitration either succeeds and commits the state
change atomically, or fails and leaves all state unchanged. There is no
partial success. This is necessary to prevent double-counting: without
atomicity, two simultaneous splits of the same parent could both succeed
while together exceeding the parent’s allocation.

==== 2.3.4 Dissolution

When an ECID’s Group is destroyed (via `ec.oe`), all Contracts held by
that Group are dissolved:

[arabic]
. All child Contracts of the dissolved Contracts are themselves
dissolved recursively.
. The allocations return to the dissolved Contracts’ parent Contracts,
making them available for re-splitting.
. If the dissolved Contract was a root Contract, its allocation returns
to the hardware resource pool.

'''''

=== 2.4 Delegation

==== 2.4.1 What delegation means

Delegation transfers resources from a parent ECID’s Group to a child
ECID’s Group. After delegation:

* The delegated resource’s owner field is updated from the parent ECID
to the child ECID.
* The parent ECID *no longer has access* to the delegated resource. It
cannot issue `ec.ib` against a Bank it has delegated away, and it cannot
use a Contract it has delegated away.

*Bank delegation* uses `ec.it` (Chapter 3 §3.4): one bank per call,
parent ECID in `rs1`, child ECID in `rs2`. To delegate N banks, call
`ec.it` N times.

*Contract delegation* is handled by the extension that owns the
Contract: `ms.it` for MSE Contracts, `qs.it` for QoS Contracts (charter
§4.3.6). `ec.it` does not transfer Contracts.

Neither instruction takes a Group ID as a separate argument, because
GroupID = ECID.

==== 2.4.2 Creating a child ECID

Delegation presupposes a child ECID. The instruction `ec.ir` (Chapter 3
§3.5) allocates a new child ECID in the calling context’s radix-tree
prefix. The kernel then:

[arabic]
. Sets `EC[child].ecs_ptr` to point to the child’s ECS in
RAM.
. Sets `EC[child].delegation_L` to `parent_L {plus} 1`.
. Sets `EC[child].parent_ecid` to the parent ECID number.
. Uses `ec.it` (once per bank) to transfer Banks to the child’s Group.
Uses `ms.it`, `qs.it`, or the appropriate extension instruction to
transfer Contracts.

Steps 1–3 are kernel software operations (writes to the `EC[e]`
array via the kernel’s privileged view). Step 4 issues one `ec.it` call
per bank being delegated; each call is O(1).

==== 2.4.3 Delegation-level invariants

Delegation depth is bounded by the implementation’s cap `D`, where
`D ≤ 3` (charter §5.1). The four levels support the realistic
virtualization hierarchy: L0 host kernel, L1 hypervisor, L2 nested
hypervisor, L3 guest.

[width="100%",cols="14%,86%",options="header",]
|===
|Condition |Meaning
|`L ++<++ D` |This ECID may create child ECIDs and delegate Banks and
Contracts.

|`L = D` |This ECID may bind resources for itself but may not delegate.
|===

An attempt to create a child ECID from an ECID at level `L = D` raises a
trap or returns an error code in `rd` (charter §6.6). The parent’s
delegation level `L` is stored in `EC[parent].delegation_L`
and is checked by hardware at the time of `ec.ir` and `ec.it`.

Contract trees are bounded by the same D. A Contract created at level
`L` can be split into children only if `L ++<++ D`. This ensures the
Contract hierarchy never exceeds four levels, bounding the worst-case
dissolution walk.

==== 2.4.4 After delegation

Once resources are delegated, the following hold:

[arabic]
. The child ECID’s Group owns the delegated resources. The parent’s
Group does not.
. The parent cannot save to, restore from, or modify a delegated Bank.
The bank’s owner field no longer matches the parent’s ECID.
. The parent cannot use a delegated Contract to request bandwidth or
latency guarantees. The Contract’s owner field no longer matches the
parent’s ECID.
. The child may further delegate (if `L ++<++ D`) or use resources for
itself (if `L = D`).
. The parent retains its own non-delegated Banks and Contracts.

'''''

=== 2.5 Revocation

==== 2.5.1 Cooperative revocation: `ec.ot`

`ec.ot rd, rs1` revokes all resources from child ECID `rs1` and returns
them to the parent’s Group (`rd` receives 0 on success or an error
code). After `ec.ot`:

* All Banks owned by ECID `rs1`’s Group have their owner fields updated
to the parent ECID.
* All Contracts held by ECID `rs1`’s Group are transferred to the parent
Group.
* Any grandchild ECIDs (descendants of `rs1`) have their resources
recursively revoked and returned up the hierarchy.
* ECID `rs1` itself remains valid in `EC[e]` — it is now an ECID
with no resources. The kernel may destroy it with `ec.oe` or reassign
resources to it.

Cooperative revocation is the normal teardown path: the parent decides
the child is done, revokes its resources, and then destroys the child
ECID via `ec.oe`.

==== 2.5.2 Forced destruction: `ec.oe`

`ec.oe rd, rs1` destroys ECID `rs1` and its entire subtree, reclaiming all
resources. `rd` receives the count of ECIDs freed; use `x0` to discard. This instruction always succeeds. The target context cannot
stall, block, or defend against it. See Chapter 3 §3.5 for the
step-by-step semantics.

`ec.oe` is the path for:

* Hostile or zombie contexts that will not cooperate with `ec.ot`.
* Fast teardown of an entire tenant subtree in one operation.
* Error recovery when a context has reached an unrecoverable state.

After `ec.oe`, all `EC[e]` slots in the destroyed subtree have
their generation counters incremented. Any software holding references
to `(hart_id, ECID, generation)` triples in the destroyed subtree
will detect the stale references on next use.

==== 2.5.3 Resource reclamation path

After `ec.oe`:

[arabic]
. All Banks formerly owned by the destroyed subtree are returned to the
*free pool* if they were leaf resources, or to the nearest surviving
ancestor’s Group if a partial subtree was destroyed.
. All Contracts formerly held by the destroyed subtree are dissolved
upward, with allocations returning to parent Contracts as described in
§2.3.4.
. All `EC[e]` slots in the destroyed subtree are marked free
with incremented generation counters.

This reclamation is complete and requires no subsequent cleanup by the
kernel, other than updating its own software data structures (ECS
objects, radix-tree bookkeeping in RAM).

'''''

=== 2.6 Invariants

The following invariants must hold at all times in a conforming
implementation. They are checked at the time of each `ec.ig`, `ec.og`,
`ec.it`, `ec.ot`, `ec.oe`, and `ec.ir` operation. Violations raise a
defined trap or return a documented failure code; silent ignore is
prohibited (charter §6.6).

[arabic]
. *Unique Bank ownership.* No Bank may have more than one owner ECID.
. *Unique Contract ownership.* No Contract may have more than one owner
ECID.
. *Contract sub-sum.* For any Contract `C` with children
`C_1..C_n`: sum of children’s allocations ≤ `C`’s allocation.
. *Delegation depth.* No ECID may have delegation level `L ++>++ D`. No
child ECID may have delegation level less than or equal to its parent’s
level.
. *Leaf cannot delegate.* An ECID at level `L = D` may not issue `ec.ir`
or `ec.it`.
. *Forced destroy always succeeds.* `ec.oe` raises no failure trap and
is never blocked by the target’s state. `ec.oe` of an already- destroyed
ECID is a no-op (not an error).
. *Parent-only modification.* Only the parent (or a privileged ancestor)
may issue `ec.it`, `ec.ot`, or `ec.oe` targeting a given child ECID. An
unprivileged ECID cannot modify resources in a sibling’s or ancestor’s
Group.

'''''

=== 2.7 Error and Trap Semantics

Operations that violate the invariants in §2.6, reference an unallocated
or stale ECID slot, or exceed the caller’s privilege level must result
in one of:

* A defined trap delivered to the calling privilege level, or
* A documented non-zero error code returned in `rd` or `cme_status`.

Silent ignore is prohibited. The specific trap codes and
`cme_status` encodings are defined in Chapter 0 §0.8.

The one exception is `ec.oe` applied to an already-destroyed ECID: this
is a no-op and not an error. The generation counter of a destroyed slot
already reflects the destruction; there is nothing to undo.

'''''

=== 2.8 Relationship to Radix Tree

The radix tree (Appendix A) is the kernel-side data structure that backs
ECID allocation and the delegation hierarchy. It is not an architectural
construct visible to the hardware. The hardware sees `EC[e]` —
an array indexed by ECID number with a fixed-stride layout. The radix
tree populates and de-populates `EC[e]` entries; hardware
queries `EC[e]` directly.

The radix tree provides two properties that the hardware `EC[e]`
array alone does not:

[arabic]
. *Prefix ownership.* A tenant owns an entire subtree of ECID numbers.
Allocations within a prefix do not require cross-tenant coordination.
. *Fast forced revocation.* Destroying a subtree rooted at ECID `e`
traverses the radix tree in O(log N) steps, collecting all descendant
ECIDs to be freed.

The detailed radix-tree data structure and the allocation, delegation,
and forced-revocation algorithms are in Appendix A.
